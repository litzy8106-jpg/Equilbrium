Part A · Lesson 1

Reversible reactions and dynamic equilibrium

What

Many reactions go to completion, stopping when one reactant runs out. Others do not, because the products can react to re-form the reactants. A reversible reaction runs in both the forward (left → right) and reverse (right → left) directions, shown by a double arrow ⇌.

H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g)   ·   N2O4(g) ⇌ 2NO2(g)

Chemical equilibrium is reached when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction. Concentrations then stop changing, but both reactions keep going. That is why it is called a dynamic equilibrium.

How

Your handout's characteristics of a system at equilibrium:

  • It is a dynamic process: forward and reverse reactions continue indefinitely.
  • It can only be reached in a closed system (nothing added or removed). In an open flask, escaping gas would keep pulling the reaction one way.
  • The final equilibrium position is the same whether you start from reactants or products. Pure N2O4 or pure NO2, the system ends in the same mixture of both (at the same T and total composition).
  • The macroscopic properties (color, pressure, concentrations) stay constant.

In the handout's graph, [N2O4] falls and [NO2] rises until they level off at 0.75 M and 0.50 M. The flat lines mark equilibrium.

Why

At the start, there is lots of N2O4 and no NO2, so the forward rate (kf[N2O4]) is high and the reverse rate (kr[NO2]2) is zero. As N2O4 is used up, the forward rate drops; as NO2 builds up, the reverse rate rises. They must meet. When they do, every N2O4 that splits is matched by two NO2 that recombine, so nothing changes overall.

Setting ratef = rater gives kf[N2O4] = kr[NO2]2, so kf/kr = [NO2]2/[N2O4] = K. The equilibrium constant is a ratio of two rate constants, which is why it depends only on temperature.

Common misconceptionEqual rates do not mean equal concentrations. In most equilibria the concentrations are quite different (0.75 M vs 0.50 M in the example).
Part A · Lesson 2

The law of mass action: Kc, Kp, and Q

What

For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, at equilibrium:

Kc = [C]c[D]d / [A]a[B]b   (molar concentrations; solutions or gases)
Kp = (PC)c(PD)d / (PA)a(PB)b   (partial pressures in atm; gases)
Kp = Kc(RT)Δn   R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K, T in K, Δn = (c + d) − (a + b) for gases only
  • Homogeneous equilibrium: everything is in the same phase (all gases, or all aqueous).
  • Heterogeneous equilibrium: more than one phase. Pure solids and pure liquids are left out of K: PbS(s) + H2O(l) ⇌ Pb2+ + S2−, Kc = [Pb2+][S2−]; FeO(s) + CO(g) ⇌ Fe(s) + CO2(g), Kp = PCO2/PCO.
  • Size of K: K ≫ 1 favors products; K ≪ 1 favors reactants.
  • Kc = Kp only when Δn = 0, because (RT)0 = 1.

Reaction quotient, Q: the same expression as K, but using the concentrations at any moment. Q < K → the reaction proceeds forward; Q > K → it proceeds in reverse; Q = K → at equilibrium.

How
Handout Question 1b: Haber process, Kc = 3.5 × 108 at 25 °C. Find Kp.

N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g): Δn = 2 − (1 + 3) = −2. T = 298 K.

Kp = (3.5 × 108)(0.0821 × 298)−2 = 3.5 × 108 ÷ 598.6 = 5.8 × 105.

Handout Question 2: 2A(aq) + 3B(aq) ⇌ C(aq) + 2D(aq) with [A] = 1, [B] = 2, [C] = 3, [D] = 4 M. Find Kc.

Kc = [C][D]2/[A]2[B]3 = (3)(4)2/(1)2(2)3 = 48/8 = 6.

Handout Question 3: rocket, 2H2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2H2O(g) at 3000 K; P(H2O) = 1.0, P(H2) = 1.5 × 10−3, P(O2) = 7.6 × 10−4 atm. Find Kc.

Kp = (1.0)2/[(1.5 × 10−3)2(7.6 × 10−4)] = 5.8 × 108.

Δn = 2 − 3 = −1, so Kp = Kc(RT)−1 and Kc = Kp(RT) = (5.8 × 108)(0.0821)(3000) = 1.4 × 1011.

Handout: Kc = 0.04 for PCl5 ⇌ PCl3 + Cl2 at 250 °C. 0.20 mol PCl3 and 0.12 mol Cl2 in 4.000 L at equilibrium. Mass of PCl5?

[PCl3] = 0.20/4 = 0.05 M; [Cl2] = 0.12/4 = 0.03 M. [PCl5] = (0.05)(0.03)/0.04 = 0.0375 M.

0.0375 mol/L × 208.5 g/mol × 4 L = 31.3 g. Convert moles to molarity before using Kc.

Change: −x, −x, +2x. 50.5 = (2x)2/(1.00 − x)2. Take the square root: 7.11 = 2x/(1.00 − x), so x = 0.780.

[HI] = 1.56 M; [H2] = [I2] = 0.22 M. Check: (1.56)2/(0.22)2 ≈ 50. ✓

Why

Pure solids and liquids are left out because their "concentration" (density divided by molar mass) is fixed: adding more solid does not make it more concentrated. Their constant values are absorbed into K.

Kc and Kp differ because P = (n/V)RT = [X]RT for an ideal gas. Substituting [X]RT for every pressure leaves an extra factor of RT for each mole of gas, so the leftover is (RT)Δn.

Why constants multiply when reactions add (your past quiz question): ΔG° values of added reactions add, and ΔG° = −RT ln K. So −RT ln Ktotal = −RT(ln K1 + ln K2) = −RT ln(K1K2), giving Ktotal = K1 × K2. Reversing negates ΔG°, which turns ln K into −ln K, i.e. 1/K.

Handout Question 1a–b: N2O4(g) ⇌ 2NO2(g) at 298 K, ΔG°f: NO2 51.3, N2O4 99.8 kJ/mol

ΔG° = 2(51.3) − 99.8 = +2.8 kJ/mol.

ln K = −ΔG°/RT = −2800/(8.314 × 298) = −1.130, so K = e−1.130 = 0.323. Convert kJ to J so the units cancel with R.

Handout: K = 0.51 for PCl5 decomposition at 500 K. Find ΔG°.

ΔG° = −(8.314)(500) ln(0.51) = −(4157)(−0.673) = +2800 J/mol = +2.8 kJ/mol. Positive, consistent with K < 1.

Why

ΔG° compares pure reactants with pure products in their standard states; K describes the mixture where the system actually settles. A reaction with a small positive ΔG° (like +2.8 kJ) still makes a noticeable amount of product (K = 0.32), because mixing reactants and products lowers G. Only a large positive ΔG° means "essentially no reaction." Every 5.7 kJ/mol of ΔG° at 25 °C changes K by a factor of 10.

For gas reactions, the K obtained from ΔG° is strictly Kp (standard state 1 bar). Your handout labels it Kc; follow your instructor's labeling on exams.

Part A · Lesson 4

Le Châtelier's principle

What

Le Châtelier's principle (1884): if a system at equilibrium is subjected to a stress, the position of the equilibrium shifts in the direction that relieves the stress.

Stresses and responses
StressShiftDoes K change?
Add a reactant / remove a productForward (→)No
Add a product / remove a reactantReverse (←)No
Decrease volume (increase pressure)Toward fewer moles of gasNo
Increase volume (decrease pressure)Toward more moles of gasNo

Raise temperatureEndothermic directionYes: K falls for exothermic, rises for endothermic reactions

How

Treat heat as a reactant (endothermic) or a product (exothermic), then apply the same "add → shift away" logic.

Handout lab: the cobalt chloride equilibrium
[Co(H2O)6]2+ (pink) + 4Cl− + heat ⇌ [CoCl4]2− (blue) + 6H2O
  • Add HCl (more Cl−) → shifts right → blue.
  • Add water → shifts left → pink.
  • Heat (hot water bath) → shifts right → blue; ice bath → pink. So the forward reaction is endothermic: Reaction A in your handout (heat on the reactant side) matches the observed colors.
  • Add AgNO3 → AgCl precipitates, removing Cl− → shifts left → pink.

Why HCl, and can NaCl or KCl be used? All three supply the common ion Cl−, so in principle they push the equilibrium the same way. Concentrated HCl (about 12 M) delivers a very high [Cl−] in a few drops, giving a dramatic color change. Solid NaCl or KCl is limited by solubility (about 6 M at most) and dissolves slowly, so the shift is smaller and slower. H+ itself plays no part in this equilibrium.

Practical application: a humidity (moisture) indicator, such as cobalt chloride paper or the blue silica-gel beads in packaging, which turn pink when they absorb water.

Handout lab: chromate–dichromate
2CrO42− (yellow) + 2H+ ⇌ Cr2O72− (orange) + H2O

Add acid (H+) → shifts right → orange. Add base: OH− removes H+ → shifts left → yellow. It is the common-ion idea with H+.

Haber process: N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3, ΔH = −92 kJ

High pressure favors NH3 (4 mol gas → 2). Low temperature favors NH3 (exothermic), but makes the reaction too slow, so industry compromises at about 400–450 °C with an iron catalyst and removes NH3 as it forms.

Why

Le Châtelier is a shortcut for comparing Q with K. Adding a reactant lowers Q below K, so the reaction runs forward until Q = K again. Squeezing a gas mixture changes every partial pressure by the same factor, but because the side with more gas moles has more pressure terms, Q changes and the system shifts toward fewer moles. Adding an inert gas at constant volume changes no partial pressure, so Q is untouched and nothing shifts.

Temperature is different: it is the only stress that changes K itself, because K = kf/kr and the endothermic direction's rate constant grows faster with temperature (larger activation energy). A catalyst lowers both activation energies equally, so it speeds both directions without moving the equilibrium.

Three definitions of acids and bases

Chemists have three definitions, and each one is wider than the one before it. You will use Brønsted–Lowry for almost every calculation in this unit, but you need all three to classify species correctly.

What
  • Arrhenius: an acid produces H+ in water; a base produces OH− in water. Limited to aqueous solutions and to bases that contain OH.
  • Brønsted–Lowry: an acid is a proton donor; a base is a proton acceptor. NH3 is a base here even though it has no OH.
  • Lewis: an acid is an electron-pair acceptor; a base is an electron-pair donor. This covers reactions with no proton at all, like BF3 + NH3 → F3B–NH3, or Fe3+ binding six water molecules.

H+ never floats around bare in water. It attaches to a water molecule as the hydronium ion, H3O+. Writing H+(aq) is shorthand for H3O+.

How

In a Brønsted–Lowry reaction, look for which species loses an H+ and which gains it. The pair that differs by exactly one H+ is a conjugate acid–base pair.

HF + H2O ⇌ F− + H3O+    pairs: HF/F− and H3O+/H2O
NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4+ + OH−    pairs: NH4+/NH3 and H2O/OH−

Water acted as a base in the first reaction and as an acid in the second. Your handout calls water amphoteric (able to act as acid or base); the more specific term for doing it by proton transfer is amphiprotic. Other amphiprotic species include HCO3−, H2PO4−, HPO42− and HS−.

At the molecular level (your handout's orbital pictures): the δ+ H of HCl is drawn toward a lone pair on water's δ− O. The H–Cl bond breaks with both electrons staying on Cl, and the bare H+ bonds to the O lone pair to form H3O+. With NH3 the roles swap: N's lone pair pulls an H+ off water, leaving OH−. In Lewis terms, water's O (or ammonia's N) is the electron-pair donor.

Why

Proton transfer is a competition between two bases for one proton. The equilibrium lies on the side of the weaker acid and weaker base, because the stronger base wins the proton. That is the reason for the conjugate rule: the stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base. HCl gives up its proton so readily that Cl− has almost no tendency to take it back, so Cl− is a negligible base. This single rule is what you will use in Lesson 11 to predict whether a salt is acidic, basic, or neutral.

Check yourself

Identify the conjugate pairs in HCO3− + OH− ⇌ CO32− + H2O.

HCO3−/CO32− and H2O/OH−. HCO3− is the acid here because it donates the proton to OH−.

Is AlCl3 an Arrhenius, Brønsted–Lowry, or Lewis acid?

Lewis only. Al has an empty orbital and accepts an electron pair (for example from Cl− or NH3), but it has no proton to donate.

Part B · Lesson 6

Water, Kw, and the pH scale

What

Pure water ionizes very slightly by transferring a proton between two water molecules (autoionization):

2 H2O(l) ⇌ H3O+(aq) + OH−(aq)    Kw = [H3O+][OH−] = 1.0 × 10−14 at 25 °C

Liquid water is left out of the expression because its concentration is essentially constant (about 55.5 M). The definitions you need:

pH = −log[H3O+]  ·  pOH = −log[OH−]  ·  pKw = pH + pOH = 14.00 (25 °C)
[H3O+] = 10−pH  ·  [OH−] = 10−pOH
How
  • Acidic: [H3O+] > [OH−]. Neutral: they are equal. Basic: [OH−] > [H3O+].
  • Know any one of [H3O+], [OH−], pH, pOH and you can get the other three.
  • Significant figures: only the digits after the decimal point in a pH count. [H+] = 2.5 × 10−4 M (2 s.f.) gives pH = 3.60 (2 decimals).
Worked example: [OH−] = 3.2 × 10−3 M. Find pH.

pOH = −log(3.2 × 10−3) = 2.49. pH = 14.00 − 2.49 = 11.51.

Or: [H3O+] = 1.0 × 10−14 / 3.2 × 10−3 = 3.1 × 10−12 M → pH 11.51. Same answer, as it must be.

Why

Because Kw is a constant, [H3O+] and [OH−] are locked in a seesaw: raise one tenfold and the other must fall tenfold. That is why you never have "zero OH−" even in strong acid.

Autoionization breaks bonds, so it is endothermic. Raising the temperature shifts it to the right and Kw grows (about 5.5 × 10−14 at 50 °C). Neutral water at 50 °C has pH ≈ 6.63, yet it is still neutral because [H3O+] = [OH−]. "Neutral" means equal concentrations, not pH 7. pH 7 is neutral only at 25 °C.

Part B · Lesson 7

Strong acids and strong bases

What

A strong acid or base ionizes essentially 100% in water, so it is a strong electrolyte. Your handout lists six strong acids: three hydrogen halides (HCl, HBr, HI) and three oxyacids (H2SO4, HNO3, HClO4). Some textbooks add chloric acid, HClO3, as a seventh. Anything not on the list, treat as weak.

No equilibrium calculation is needed. Use stoichiometry: [H3O+] equals the acid concentration, and [OH−] equals the base concentration times the number of OH− per formula unit.

Why

For HCl, the conjugate base Cl− is far weaker than water as a base, so water wins every proton and the reverse reaction practically never happens. Mg(OH)2 is fully ionic, but it is so insoluble that it never produces much OH−. That is why it works safely as milk of magnesia, while NaOH would burn your esophagus. "Strong" is about the percentage ionized, not about how much dissolves.

Part B · Lesson 8

Weak acids and Ka

What

A weak acid ionizes only partly, and an equilibrium constant describes how far:

HA + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + A−    Ka = [H3O+][A−] / [HA]    pKa = −log Ka

Larger Ka (smaller pKa) means a stronger acid. Your handout's Table 7.2, strongest to weakest: HSO4− 1.2 × 10−2, HClO2 1.2 × 10−2, monochloroacetic acid 1.35 × 10−3, HF 7.2 × 10−4, HNO2 4.0 × 10−4, acetic acid 1.8 × 10−5 (pKa 4.74), [Al(H2O)6]3+ 1.4 × 10−5, HOCl 3.5 × 10−8, HCN 6.2 × 10−10, NH4+ 5.6 × 10−10, phenol 1.6 × 10−10.

How is acid strength measured? By Ka, or more conveniently by pKa (your past quiz answer). Smaller pKa = stronger acid. For bases, use Kb/pKb, or the pKa of the conjugate acid.

Remember: pH reflects [H3O+], not [acid]. In 0.1 M HCl, [H3O+] = 0.1 M (pH 1); in 0.1 M acetic acid, [H3O+] is only about 10−3 M (pH ≈ 3), because most acid molecules never donate their proton.

How

Use an ICE table (Initial, Change, Equilibrium) and solve for x = [H3O+].

  • 5% rule: the shortcut x ≈ √(Ka·C) is valid when x/C < 5%. A quick screen: it usually works if C/Ka > 400. If it fails, solve the quadratic x² + Kax − KaC = 0.
  • Percent ionization = [H3O+]eq / [HA]initial × 100%.
  • Going backwards (finding Ka from a measured pH): [H3O+] = 10−pH = x, then Ka = x² / (C − x).
Why

Dilution increases percent ionization. At 0.10 M acetic acid is 1.3% ionized; at 0.010 M it is 4.2% ionized, even though the pH rises from 2.87 to 3.37. Why: ionization turns one particle (HA) into two (H3O+ + A−). Diluting drops the reaction quotient Q = x²/(C − x) below Ka because the numerator has two concentration terms and the denominator only one. The system shifts right to restore Ka, the same way Le Châtelier predicts that a gas reaction shifts toward more moles when volume increases. More of the acid ionizes, but each ion is more spread out, so [H3O+] still falls.

Part B · Lesson 9

Weak bases, Kb, and Ka · Kb = Kw

What

Weak bases take a proton from water. Most are ammonia, amines (N with a lone pair), or anions of weak acids.

B + H2O ⇌ BH+ + OH−    Kb = [BH+][OH−] / [B]

NH3 Kb = 1.8 × 10−5, CH3NH2 4.4 × 10−4, pyridine 1.7 × 10−9.

For any conjugate pair: Ka × Kb = Kw     pKa + pKb = 14.00
How

Solve exactly like a weak acid, but x is [OH−]. Then convert pOH to pH as the last step.

Worked example: pH of 0.15 M NH3

x² / 0.15 = 1.8 × 10−5 → x = √(2.7 × 10−6) = 1.64 × 10−3 M = [OH−]. Check: 1.1% < 5%.

pOH = 2.78 → pH = 11.22.

Handout: pH of 1.5 M NH3 (Kb = 1.8 × 10−5)

x2/1.5 = 1.8 × 10−5 → x2 = 2.7 × 10−5, x = 5.20 × 10−3 M = [OH−]. [H3O+] = 1.0 × 10−14/5.20 × 10−3 = 1.92 × 10−12 M, pH = 11.72.

Handout: for HNO2, Ka = 4.5 × 10−4, so Kb(NO2−) = 1.0 × 10−14/4.5 × 10−4 = 2.2 × 10−11.

If a table only gives Ka of HF, you can still find Kb of F−: Kb = 1.0 × 10−14 / 6.8 × 10−4 = 1.5 × 10−11.

Why

Add the two reactions: HA + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + A− (Ka) and A− + H2O ⇌ HA + OH− (Kb). HA and A− cancel and you are left with 2H2O ⇌ H3O+ + OH−, which is Kw. When reactions add, their constants multiply, so KaKb = Kw. That product is fixed, so a bigger Ka must mean a smaller Kb. It is the conjugate rule from Lesson 5, now with numbers.

Part B · Lesson 10

Molecular structure and acid strength

What

An acid is stronger when its H–X bond breaks more easily and its conjugate base A− is more stable. Three families follow three rules:

  • Binary acids, same group (down a column): bond strength controls. HF < HCl < HBr < HI. Larger atoms form longer, weaker bonds with H.
  • Binary acids, same period (across a row): electronegativity controls. CH4 < NH3 < H2O < HF. A more electronegative atom pulls electron density from H and holds the negative charge better.
  • Oxyacids (H–O–Y): (a) same number of O: more electronegative Y is stronger, HOI < HOBr < HOCl. (b) same Y: more O atoms is stronger, HClO < HClO2 < HClO3 < HClO4.
  • Carboxylic acids: the –COOH proton is acidic because the carboxylate ion is resonance-stabilized. Electronegative substituents nearby increase strength: Cl3CCOOH ≫ CH3COOH.
How

Ask which acid family you are comparing first, then apply that family's rule. The most common exam mistake is using electronegativity down a group: F is the most electronegative halogen, yet HF is the weakest hydrohalic acid.

Why

Down a group, the bond to H gets dramatically weaker (H–F about 567 kJ/mol, H–I about 299 kJ/mol), and that effect outweighs the drop in electronegativity. Across a row, atom size barely changes, so electronegativity becomes the deciding factor. In oxyacids, each extra O or a more electronegative Y pulls electron density away from the O–H bond (an inductive effect), making it more polar and easier to break. The extra O atoms also spread the negative charge of the anion over more atoms (resonance), which stabilizes the conjugate base. A more stable conjugate base is a weaker base, and therefore belongs to a stronger acid.

Conjugate bases and solvents (from your handout)

  • Acid strength = ease of removing a proton. Base strength = availability of nonbonded (lone) electron pairs.
  • Conjugate bases of binary acids: basicity decreases F− > Cl− > Br− > I−, because a larger ionic radius spreads (diffuses) the electron cloud, so the lone pairs are held less available.
  • Across a row: NH2− > OH− > F− in basicity (decreasing availability of lone pairs), mirroring NH3 < H2O < HF in acidity. Stronger acid = weaker conjugate base.
  • Conjugate bases of oxyacids: ClO− > ClO2− > ClO3− > ClO4− in basicity. More resonance structures spread the negative charge and make a more stable, weaker base.
Leveling

The leveling effect is a solvent's ability to level the strength of strong acids or bases dissolved in it. HClO4 has more O atoms and is intrinsically more acidic than HCl, but in water both ionize completely to H3O+, so they look equally strong.

  1. A strong acid is leveled to the strength of the solvent's conjugate acid (H3O+ in water).
  2. A strong base is leveled to the strength of the solvent's conjugate base (OH− in water).
  3. Strongly basic solvents level acids; weakly basic solvents are differentiating solvents that reveal differences between strong acids.

Your handout gives NH3 as the solvent that differentiates HI > HCl. By its own rule 3, though, NH3 is more basic than water and would level acids even more. The usual textbook example of a differentiating solvent for strong acids is a weakly basic one such as pure acetic acid. If this comes up, it's worth confirming with your instructor which example they expect.

An amphiprotic ion can either lose a proton (its Ka is the next Ka of the parent acid) or gain one (its Kb = Kw/the previous Ka). Whichever constant is bigger wins.

  • HCO3−: Ka = Ka2 = 5.6 × 10−11; Kb = 10−14/Ka1(4.3 × 10−7) = 2.3 × 10−8. Kb wins → baking soda is basic. pH ≈ ½(pKa1 + pKa2) = ½(6.37 + 10.25) ≈ 8.3.
  • H2PO4−: Ka = Ka2 = 6.2 × 10−8; Kb = 10−14/7.5 × 10−3 = 1.3 × 10−12 → NaH2PO4 is acidic.
  • HPO42−: Ka = Ka3 = 4.2 × 10−13; Kb = 10−14/6.2 × 10−8 = 1.6 × 10−7 → Na2HPO4 is basic.
Why

Why spectators stay silent. Cl− is the conjugate base of a strong acid. Its Kb would be Kw/(huge Ka), which is effectively zero, so it never pulls a proton from water. The same logic makes Na+ inert: NaOH is strong, so Na+ has no tendency to grab OH− back.

Why some metal ions are acidic. In water, a metal cation is surrounded by water molecules bonded through oxygen (a Lewis acid–base interaction). A small ion with a high charge, like Al3+ or Fe3+, has a high charge density. It pulls electron density out of the O–H bonds of its attached waters, which weakens them until one releases H+. [Al(H2O)6]3+ has Ka ≈ 1.4 × 10−5, about as strong as acetic acid. Na+ is too large and its charge too low to do this. The higher the charge and the smaller the radius, the more acidic the hydrated ion.

The common-ion effect

What

The common-ion effect is the shift of an ionic equilibrium caused by adding a solute that supplies an ion already in the equilibrium. Adding the common ion pushes the equilibrium toward the un-ionized (or undissolved) side.

  • Weak acid + its salt: CH3COOH + CH3COONa. The extra CH3COO− suppresses ionization, so [H3O+] drops and pH rises.
  • Weak base + its salt: NH3 + NH4Cl. The extra NH4+ suppresses ionization, so [OH−] drops and pH falls.
  • Sparingly soluble salt + a soluble salt sharing an ion: AgCl in NaCl solution. The extra Cl− lowers how much AgCl dissolves (Lesson 16).
How

Put the common ion into the Initial row of the ICE table. That one change is the whole technique.

Worked example: 0.20 M NH3 with 0.30 M NH4Cl

[OH−] = Kb[NH3]/[NH4+] = 1.8 × 10−5 × 0.20/0.30 = 1.2 × 10−5 M → pOH 4.92 → pH = 9.08. Plain 0.20 M NH3 would be pH 11.28.

Why

This is Le Châtelier's principle with a number attached. Adding A− makes Q = [H3O+][A−]/[HA] larger than Ka. To bring Q back down to Ka, the reaction runs in reverse, consuming H3O+ and A− to form HA. Ka itself does not change (only temperature changes K); what changes is the position of the equilibrium.

The common-ion effect also explains why the "x is small" approximation becomes excellent: the common ion already suppresses x, so 0.10 ± x really is 0.10. And it is the direct foundation of buffers. A solution with large amounts of both HA and A− has a pH set by their ratio, not by how far HA ionizes. Lesson 13 builds on exactly this.

Handout: H2S and added HCl

H2S(aq) ⇌ 2H+(aq) + S2−(aq). Adding HCl raises [H+] sharply. H+ and S2− recombine into undissociated H2S, so [S2−] drops dramatically. Because [S2−] depends on [H+]2, a small change in pH changes [S2−] enormously. Chemists use this in qualitative analysis to control which metal sulfides precipitate.

Not a common ionAdding NaCl to acetic acid adds no common ion, so it has essentially no effect on the acetic acid equilibrium at this level. Adding HCl does supply a common ion (H3O+), and it suppresses acetic acid ionization even more strongly than acetate does.

Buffers

What

A buffer resists changes in pH when small amounts of strong acid or strong base are added. It contains a weak acid and its conjugate base (CH3COOH/CH3COO−) or a weak base and its conjugate acid (NH3/NH4+), both in appreciable amounts.

Henderson–Hasselbalch:   pH = pKa + log ( [base] / [acid] )
  • When [base] = [acid], pH = pKa.
  • Effective range: pKa ± 1, which is where the base/acid ratio is between 1:10 and 10:1.
  • Buffer capacity is how much acid or base it can absorb before the pH changes much. It depends on the absolute amounts of HA and A−, not just their ratio, and it is highest when the ratio is 1:1.
  • Not buffers: strong acid + its salt (HCl + NaCl), or a weak acid alone. A strong acid's conjugate (Cl−) cannot neutralize added H+, and a weak acid alone has no base reservoir.
How

Choosing a buffer: pick a weak acid whose pKa is closest to the target pH, then set the ratio with Henderson–Hasselbalch.

Adding strong acid or base is a two-step problem. Step 1: stoichiometry, in moles. The strong reagent reacts completely. Step 2: equilibrium, using Henderson–Hasselbalch with the new moles (the volume cancels in the ratio).

Worked example: add 0.010 mol HCl to 1.00 L of 0.25 M CH3COOH / 0.25 M CH3COONa

Start: pH = 4.74 + log(0.25/0.25) = 4.74.

Stoichiometry: H+ + CH3COO− → CH3COOH. Acetate 0.25 → 0.24 mol; acetic acid 0.25 → 0.26 mol.

Equilibrium: pH = 4.74 + log(0.24/0.26) = 4.74 − 0.035 = 4.71. It changed by only 0.03.

The same 0.010 mol HCl in 1.00 L of pure water gives pH 2.00, a drop of 5 units.

Worked example: what ratio gives pH 5.00 with acetic acid?

5.00 = 4.74 + log(b/a) → log(b/a) = 0.26 → b/a = 100.26 = 1.8. You need 1.8 mol of acetate for every mole of acetic acid.

Why

A buffer has a reservoir on each side. Added H+ is captured by the base (A− + H+ → HA). Added OH− is captured by the acid (HA + OH− → A− + H2O). The strong reagent is converted into the weak partner, and pH depends only on the logarithm of a ratio. With large amounts of both, a small addition barely changes the ratio, and the log flattens that change even further. Henderson–Hasselbalch is just Ka rearranged: take −log of [H3O+] = Ka[HA]/[A−] and you get it. The ±1 range follows because outside 10:1 one reservoir becomes too small to absorb anything.

Diluting a buffer with water does not change its pH (the ratio is unchanged), but it does lower its capacity.

For a basic buffer (weak base + its salt), your handout uses the pOH form: pOH = pKb + log([conjugate acid]/[weak base]), then pH = 14 − pOH. The two assumptions behind both forms: [weak acid] ≈ concentration of acid used (it barely dissociates), and [conjugate base] ≈ concentration of salt used (the salt fully dissociates, and the acid adds negligible A−).

Handout examples: three buffer calculations

3.20 g sodium ethanoate (Mr 82) in 1.00 L of 0.01 M ethanoic acid, Ka = 1.75 × 10−5: [salt] = 3.20/82 = 0.039 M. pH = −log(1.75 × 10−5) + log(0.039/0.01) = 4.76 + 0.59 = 5.35.

300 mL of 0.40 M NH3 + 200 mL of 0.45 M NH4Cl, Kb = 1.8 × 10−5: after mixing (500 mL), [NH3] = 0.40 × 300/500 = 0.24 M and [NH4+] = 0.45 × 200/500 = 0.18 M. pOH = 4.74 + log(0.18/0.24) = 4.62, so pH = 9.38. Dilute both to the final volume, or just use moles: the volume cancels.

500 mL of 2.0 M butanoic acid + 500 mL of 0.50 M sodium butanoate (Ka = 1.50 × 10−5), then 100 mL of 0.50 M HCl: moles: acid 1.0, base 0.25. Before: pH = 4.82 + log(0.25/1.0) = 4.22. HCl adds 0.05 mol H+: base 0.25 → 0.20, acid 1.0 → 1.05. After: pH = 4.82 + log(0.20/1.05) = 4.10. Change = 0.12, only a small change, as a buffer should give.

Why

Why Ka1 > Ka2 > Ka3, always. The first proton leaves a neutral molecule. The second must leave an ion that is already negative, and the third leaves a 2− ion. Pulling a positive proton away from an increasingly negative species takes more and more energy, because of electrostatic attraction. On top of that, the H3O+ already produced by step 1 is a common ion that suppresses step 2. Both effects push the later steps far to the left.

Will it precipitate? Compute the ion product Q with the concentrations right after mixing (remember dilution). Q > Ksp: precipitate forms. Q = Ksp: saturated. Q < Ksp: no precipitate.

Worked example: mix equal volumes of 0.010 M BaCl2 and 0.010 M Na2SO4 (Ksp BaSO4 = 1.1 × 10−10)

Mixing halves each: [Ba2+] = [SO42−] = 0.0050 M. Q = (0.0050)² = 2.5 × 10−5 ≫ 1.1 × 10−10, so BaSO4 precipitates.

Handout: CaSO4 mixing problems

(a) Equal volumes of 1 × 10−3 M Ca2+ and SO42−: each becomes 5 × 10−4 M. Q = 2.5 × 10−7, below Ksp (2 × 10−5), so no precipitate.

(b) 25 mL of 0.01 M Ca2+ + 50 mL of 0.02 M SO42−: [Ca2+] = 3.33 × 10−3, [SO42−] = 0.0133 M. Q = 4.43 × 10−5 > 2 × 10−5, so CaSO4 precipitates until Q falls to Ksp.

Mass precipitated from 125 mL of 1.00 M H2SO4 + 125 mL of 1.00 M Ca(NO3)2 (Ksp = 6.0 × 10−5): after mixing [Ca2+] = 0.500 M; at saturation x = √(6.0 × 10−5) = 7.75 × 10−3 M remains. Precipitated: (0.500 − 0.00775) × 0.250 L = 0.123 mol × 136.2 g/mol = 16.7 g.

Handout: keeping a salt from precipitating, AgOH (Ksp = 1.5 × 10−8) in 0.010 M AgNO3

Precipitation starts when [Ag+][OH−] = Ksp: [OH−] = 1.5 × 10−8/0.010 = 1.5 × 10−6 M, pOH = 5.82, pH ≈ 8.2 (the handout rounds to 8.17). At this pH the solution is just saturated; keep the pH below it to prevent AgOH from forming.

Handout: selective precipitation of Au+ and Ag+ (0.0010 M each) with NaCl. Ksp: AgCl 1.8 × 10−10, AuCl 2.0 × 10−13

Same formula type, so the lower Ksp precipitates first: AuCl.

[Cl−] to start AuCl: 2.0 × 10−13/0.0010 = 2.0 × 10−10 M. To start AgCl: 1.8 × 10−10/0.0010 = 1.8 × 10−7 M.

When AgCl begins, [Au+] = 2.0 × 10−13/1.8 × 10−7 = 1.1 × 10−6 M, so only 0.11% of the gold is left in solution. The separation is nearly complete.

Handout: 0.10 M Na2CO3 and 0.10 M Na2SO4; add 0.10 mol CaCl2 to 1.0 L. Ksp: CaCO3 1.69 × 10−8, CaSO4 2.3 × 10−4

(a) Same formula type; CaCO3 has the lower Ksp, so it precipitates first.

(b) 0.10 mol Ca2+ meets 0.10 mol CO32−. At saturation [Ca2+] = √(1.69 × 10−8) = 1.3 × 10−4 M remains, so CaCO3 precipitated = 0.10 − 0.00013 = 0.0999 mol.

Does CaSO4 also precipitate? Q = (1.3 × 10−4)(0.10) = 1.3 × 10−5 < 2.3 × 10−4, so no.

Selective precipitation: 0.010 M Cl− and 0.010 M CrO42−, add Ag+ slowly

AgCl begins when [Ag+] = 1.8 × 10−10/0.010 = 1.8 × 10−8 M. Ag2CrO4 (Ksp 1.1 × 10−12) begins when [Ag+] = √(1.1 × 10−12/0.010) = 1.0 × 10−5 M. AgCl precipitates first. The red Ag2CrO4 appears only after nearly all Cl− is gone, which is how the Mohr method detects the end point of a chloride titration.

Why

Compare Ksp values directly only for the same formula type. Ag2CrO4 has a smaller Ksp (1.1 × 10−12) than AgCl (1.8 × 10−10) but is more soluble: s = (Ksp/4)1/3 = 6.5 × 10−5 M versus 1.3 × 10−5 M. The exponents in the expression differ, so the constants are not on the same scale.

pH changes solubility when the anion is basic. In Mg(OH)2, CaCO3, CaF2 or Ca3(PO4)2, added H+ removes the anion (OH− → H2O, CO32− → HCO3− → CO2). Q drops below Ksp, so more solid dissolves. Salts of strong-acid anions (AgCl, BaSO4) are barely affected by pH, because Cl− has no basicity to remove.

Complexing agents (your handout): a metal complex forms from a metal ion (a Lewis acid) and ligands (Lewis bases). Its equilibrium constant is the formation (stability) constant, Kf = [Ag(NH3)2+]/[Ag+][NH3]2. Adding a complexing agent keeps a metal cation from precipitating, because the complex is more soluble than the metal salt.

0.0050 M AgNO3 in 2.00 M NH3 (Kf = 1.7 × 107): essentially all Ag+ becomes complex (0.0050 M), leaving 1.99 M NH3. Then Kd = 1/Kf = x(1.99)2/0.0050 gives [Ag+] = 7.4 × 10−11 M.

AgBr in (a) water and (b) 0.10 M NH3 (Ksp = 5.0 × 10−13, Kf = 1.7 × 107): (a) s = √(5.0 × 10−13) = 7.1 × 10−7 M. (b) Add the equations: AgBr(s) + 2NH3 ⇌ Ag(NH3)2+ + Br−, K = Ksp × Kf = 8.5 × 10−6 = x2/(0.10 − 2x)2, so x = 2.9 × 10−4 M, about 400 times more soluble.

Complex ions also raise solubility. AgCl dissolves in aqueous NH3 because Ag+ + 2NH3 ⇌ Ag(NH3)2+ (Kf = 1.7 × 107) removes free Ag+. Amphoteric hydroxides such as Al(OH)3, Zn(OH)2 and Cr(OH)3 dissolve in both strong acid and excess strong base, the latter by forming complexes like Al(OH)4−.

Health linkTooth enamel is hydroxyapatite, Ca5(PO4)3OH. Acid from bacteria feeding on sugar removes OH− and PO43−, so enamel dissolves (caries). Fluoride swaps in for OH− to form fluorapatite, which is less soluble and has a less basic anion, so it resists acid. BaSO4 can be swallowed for X-ray imaging even though Ba2+ is toxic, because its Ksp (1.1 × 10−10) keeps free Ba2+ extremely low, and its non-basic sulfate anion means stomach acid barely dissolves it.

Memory hooks

Writing K

"Products over reactants; solids and liquids sit out"

Raise each concentration to its coefficient. Pure solids and liquids (including water as solvent) never appear in K.

Kc ↔ Kp

Kp = Kc(RT)Δn

Δn counts gas moles only, products minus reactants. R = 0.0821. If Δn = 0, they are equal.

Manipulating K

Flip → 1/K · ×n → Kn · Add → multiply

Because ΔG° = −RT ln K: flipping negates ΔG°, scaling scales it, and adding ΔG° values multiplies the Ks.

ΔG° and K

"Negative G, big K"

ΔG° < 0 ↔ K > 1 (products). ΔG° > 0 ↔ K < 1. About 5.7 kJ/mol of ΔG° = a factor of 10 in K at 25 °C.

Le Châtelier

"Push it, it pushes back"

Add → shift away. Remove → shift toward. Squeeze → fewer gas moles. Heat → endothermic side. Only temperature changes K.

Cobalt chloride

"Blue when hot and salty, pink when wet and cold"

[CoCl4]2− (blue) is favored by heat and Cl−; [Co(H2O)6]2+ (pink) by water and cooling. Used in humidity indicators.

The six strong acids (handout)

"Clara Brought Iced Sodas, No Peanuts"

HCl · HBr · HI · H2SO4 · HNO3 · HClO4 (Perchloric). Three hydrogen halides + three oxyacids. Some books add HClO3. HF is weak.

Strong bases

All of Group 1, plus "Ca·Sr·Ba"

Read it as "Can Sara Bake?" Group 2 hydroxides from Ca down. Mg(OH)2 and Be(OH)2 are too insoluble to count.

Henderson–Hasselbalch

B·O·A

Base Over Acid: pH = pKa + log(base/acid). More base pushes pH above pKa; more acid pulls it below.

Salts

"A salt takes after its stronger parent"

NH4Cl comes from weak NH3 and strong HCl, so it is acidic. NaF comes from strong NaOH and weak HF, so it is basic. Two strong parents: neutral. Two weak parents: compare Ka and Kb.

Conjugates

The seesaw: Ka × Kb = 10−14

One end goes up, the other goes down. A strong acid has a uselessly weak conjugate base; a very weak acid has a fairly strong one.

Half-equivalence

"Halfway there, pH is pKa"

Half the acid has become its conjugate base, the ratio is 1, log 1 = 0. This is how pKa is measured in the lab.

Indicator choice

Weak acid → pink · Weak base → red

Titrating a weak acid? The equivalence point is basic, so use phenolphthalein (turns pink). Titrating a weak base? It's acidic, so use methyl red.

Common-ion effect

"Add a product, push it back"

Supplying an ion that appears on the product side drives the equilibrium toward reactants: less ionization, less dissolving.

Polyprotic acids

"Each proton is harder to pull"

It leaves an ion that is more negative each time, so Ka1 ≫ Ka2 ≫ Ka3. First step sets the pH; [A2−] ≈ Ka2.

Ksp shortcuts

1:1 → s² · 1:2 → 4s³ · 1:3 → 27s⁴

Multiply the coefficient raised to its own power for each ion: (2s)² = 4s², times s = 4s³.

Precipitation

"Q over K, ions fall away"

If Q > Ksp, there are too many ions in solution; solid falls out until Q = Ksp.

Neutral water

"Neutral means equal, not seven"

pH 7 is neutral only at 25 °C. Warmer water has a lower neutral pH but is still neutral.
