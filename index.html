Lesson 1

Three definitions of acids and bases

Chemists have three definitions, and each one is wider than the one before it. You will use Brønsted–Lowry for almost every calculation in this unit, but you need all three to classify species correctly.

What
  • Arrhenius: an acid produces H+ in water; a base produces OH− in water. Limited to aqueous solutions and to bases that contain OH.
  • Brønsted–Lowry: an acid is a proton donor; a base is a proton acceptor. NH3 is a base here even though it has no OH.
  • Lewis: an acid is an electron-pair acceptor; a base is an electron-pair donor. This covers reactions with no proton at all, like BF3 + NH3 → F3B–NH3, or Fe3+ binding six water molecules.

H+ never floats around bare in water. It attaches to a water molecule as the hydronium ion, H3O+. Writing H+(aq) is shorthand for H3O+.

How

In a Brønsted–Lowry reaction, look for which species loses an H+ and which gains it. The pair that differs by exactly one H+ is a conjugate acid–base pair.

HF + H2O ⇌ F− + H3O+    pairs: HF/F− and H3O+/H2O
NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4+ + OH−    pairs: NH4+/NH3 and H2O/OH−

Water acted as a base in the first reaction and as an acid in the second. Your handout calls water amphoteric (able to act as acid or base); the more specific term for doing it by proton transfer is amphiprotic. Other amphiprotic species include HCO3−, H2PO4−, HPO42− and HS−.

At the molecular level (your handout's orbital pictures): the δ+ H of HCl is drawn toward a lone pair on water's δ− O. The H–Cl bond breaks with both electrons staying on Cl, and the bare H+ bonds to the O lone pair to form H3O+. With NH3 the roles swap: N's lone pair pulls an H+ off water, leaving OH−. In Lewis terms, water's O (or ammonia's N) is the electron-pair donor.

Why

Proton transfer is a competition between two bases for one proton. The equilibrium lies on the side of the weaker acid and weaker base, because the stronger base wins the proton. That is the reason for the conjugate rule: the stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base. HCl gives up its proton so readily that Cl− has almost no tendency to take it back, so Cl− is a negligible base. This single rule is what you will use in Lesson 7 to predict whether a salt is acidic, basic, or neutral.

Check yourself

Identify the conjugate pairs in HCO3− + OH− ⇌ CO32− + H2O.

HCO3−/CO32− and H2O/OH−. HCO3− is the acid here because it donates the proton to OH−.

Lesson 2

Water, Kw, and the pH scale

What

Pure water ionizes very slightly by transferring a proton between two water molecules (autoionization):

2 H2O(l) ⇌ H3O+(aq) + OH−(aq)    Kw = [H3O+][OH−] = 1.0 × 10−14 at 25 °C

Liquid water is left out of the expression because its concentration is essentially constant (about 55.5 M). The definitions you need:

pH = −log[H3O+]  ·  pOH = −log[OH−]  ·  pKw = pH + pOH = 14.00 (25 °C)
[H3O+] = 10−pH  ·  [OH−] = 10−pOH
How
  • Acidic: [H3O+] > [OH−]. Neutral: they are equal. Basic: [OH−] > [H3O+].
  • Know any one of [H3O+], [OH−], pH, pOH and you can get the other three.
  • Significant figures: only the digits after the decimal point in a pH count. [H+] = 2.5 × 10−4 M (2 s.f.) gives pH = 3.60 (2 decimals).
Worked example: [OH−] = 3.2 × 10−3 M. Find pH.

pOH = −log(3.2 × 10−3) = 2.49. pH = 14.00 − 2.49 = 11.51.

Or: [H3O+] = 1.0 × 10−14 / 3.2 × 10−3 = 3.1 × 10−12 M → pH 11.51. Same answer, as it must be.

Why

Because Kw is a constant, [H3O+] and [OH−] are locked in a seesaw: raise one tenfold and the other must fall tenfold. That is why you never have "zero OH−" even in strong acid.

Autoionization breaks bonds, so it is endothermic. Raising the temperature shifts it to the right and Kw grows (about 5.5 × 10−14 at 50 °C). Neutral water at 50 °C has pH ≈ 6.63, yet it is still neutral because [H3O+] = [OH−]. "Neutral" means equal concentrations, not pH 7. pH 7 is neutral only at 25 °C.

Lesson 3

Strong acids and strong bases

What

A strong acid or base ionizes essentially 100% in water, so it is a strong electrolyte. Your handout lists six strong acids: three hydrogen halides (HCl, HBr, HI) and three oxyacids (H2SO4, HNO3, HClO4). Some textbooks add chloric acid, HClO3, as a seventh. Anything not on the list, treat as weak.

Strong acids (handout's six, plus HClO3 in some books) and strong bases
Strong acidsStrong bases
HCl, HBr, HI (hydrogen halides, except HF)
H2SO4 (first proton), HNO3, HClO4

Heavy group 2 hydroxides: Ca(OH)2, Sr(OH)2, Ba(OH)2
How

No equilibrium calculation is needed. Use stoichiometry: [H3O+] equals the acid concentration, and [OH−] equals the base concentration times the number of OH− per formula unit.

0.020 M HNO3 → [H3O+] = 0.020 M → pH = 1.70
0.0050 M Ba(OH)2 → [OH−] = 2 × 0.0050 = 0.010 M → pOH 2.00 → pH 12.00
Handout: pH of 2.0 × 10−3 M Ba(OH)2

Ba(OH)2 → Ba2+ + 2OH−, so [OH−] = 4.0 × 10−3 M. [H+] = 1.0 × 10−14/4.0 × 10−3 = 2.5 × 10−12 M, pH = 11.60.

Likewise, in 0.100 M Ba(OH)2: 0.00 M Ba(OH)2 remains, [Ba2+] = 0.100 M, [OH−] = 0.200 M.

Why

For HCl, the conjugate base Cl− is far weaker than water as a base, so water wins every proton and the reverse reaction practically never happens. Mg(OH)2 is fully ionic, but it is so insoluble that it never produces much OH−. That is why it works safely as milk of magnesia, while NaOH would burn your esophagus. "Strong" is about the percentage ionized, not about how much dissolves.

Lesson 4

Weak acids and Ka

What

A weak acid ionizes only partly, and an equilibrium constant describes how far:

HA + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + A−    Ka = [H3O+][A−] / [HA]    pKa = −log Ka

Larger Ka (smaller pKa) means a stronger acid. Your handout's Table 7.2, strongest to weakest: HSO4− 1.2 × 10−2, HClO2 1.2 × 10−2, monochloroacetic acid 1.35 × 10−3, HF 7.2 × 10−4, HNO2 4.0 × 10−4, acetic acid 1.8 × 10−5 (pKa 4.74), [Al(H2O)6]3+ 1.4 × 10−5, HOCl 3.5 × 10−8, HCN 6.2 × 10−10, NH4+ 5.6 × 10−10, phenol 1.6 × 10−10.

How is acid strength measured? By Ka, or more conveniently by pKa (your past quiz answer). Smaller pKa = stronger acid. For bases, use Kb/pKb, or the pKa of the conjugate acid.

Remember: pH reflects [H3O+], not [acid]. In 0.1 M HCl, [H3O+] = 0.1 M (pH 1); in 0.1 M acetic acid, [H3O+] is only about 10−3 M (pH ≈ 3), because most acid molecules never donate their proton.

How

Use an ICE table (Initial, Change, Equilibrium) and solve for x = [H3O+].

Worked example: pH of 0.10 M acetic acid (Ka = 1.8 × 10−5)

  • 5% rule: the shortcut x ≈ √(Ka·C) is valid when x/C < 5%. A quick screen: it usually works if C/Ka > 400. If it fails, solve the quadratic x² + Kax − KaC = 0.
  • Percent ionization = [H3O+]eq / [HA]initial × 100%.
  • Going backwards (finding Ka from a measured pH): [H3O+] = 10−pH = x, then Ka = x² / (C − x).
Why

Dilution increases percent ionization. At 0.10 M acetic acid is 1.3% ionized; at 0.010 M it is 4.2% ionized, even though the pH rises from 2.87 to 3.37. Why: ionization turns one particle (HA) into two (H3O+ + A−). Diluting drops the reaction quotient Q = x²/(C − x) below Ka because the numerator has two concentration terms and the denominator only one. The system shifts right to restore Ka, the same way Le Châtelier predicts that a gas reaction shifts toward more moles when volume increases. More of the acid ionizes, but each ion is more spread out, so [H3O+] still falls.

Lesson 1

Weak bases, Kb, and Ka · Kb = Kw

What

Weak bases take a proton from water. Most are ammonia, amines (N with a lone pair), or anions of weak acids.

B + H2O ⇌ BH+ + OH−    Kb = [BH+][OH−] / [B]

NH3 Kb = 1.8 × 10−5, CH3NH2 4.4 × 10−4, pyridine 1.7 × 10−9.

For any conjugate pair: Ka × Kb = Kw     pKa + pKb = 14.00
How

Solve exactly like a weak acid, but x is [OH−]. Then convert pOH to pH as the last step.

Worked example: pH of 0.15 M NH3

x² / 0.15 = 1.8 × 10−5 → x = √(2.7 × 10−6) = 1.64 × 10−3 M = [OH−]. Check: 1.1% < 5%.

pOH = 2.78 → pH = 11.22.

Handout: pH of 1.5 M NH3 (Kb = 1.8 × 10−5)

x2/1.5 = 1.8 × 10−5 → x2 = 2.7 × 10−5, x = 5.20 × 10−3 M = [OH−]. [H3O+] = 1.0 × 10−14/5.20 × 10−3 = 1.92 × 10−12 M, pH = 11.72.

Handout: for HNO2, Ka = 4.5 × 10−4, so Kb(NO2−) = 1.0 × 10−14/4.5 × 10−4 = 2.2 × 10−11.

If a table only gives Ka of HF, you can still find Kb of F−: Kb = 1.0 × 10−14 / 6.8 × 10−4 = 1.5 × 10−11.

Why

Add the two reactions: HA + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + A− (Ka) and A− + H2O ⇌ HA + OH− (Kb). HA and A− cancel and you are left with 2H2O ⇌ H3O+ + OH−, which is Kw. When reactions add, their constants multiply, so KaKb = Kw. That product is fixed, so a bigger Ka must mean a smaller Kb. It is the conjugate rule from Lesson 1, now with numbers.

Lesson 2

Molecular structure and acid strength

What

An acid is stronger when its H–X bond breaks more easily and its conjugate base A− is more stable. Three families follow three rules:

  • Binary acids, same group (down a column): bond strength controls. HF < HCl < HBr < HI. Larger atoms form longer, weaker bonds with H.
  • Binary acids, same period (across a row): electronegativity controls. CH4 < NH3 < H2O < HF. A more electronegative atom pulls electron density from H and holds the negative charge better.
  • Oxyacids (H–O–Y): (a) same number of O: more electronegative Y is stronger, HOI < HOBr < HOCl. (b) same Y: more O atoms is stronger, HClO < HClO2 < HClO3 < HClO4.
  • Carboxylic acids: the –COOH proton is acidic because the carboxylate ion is resonance-stabilized. Electronegative substituents nearby increase strength: Cl3CCOOH ≫ CH3COOH.
How

Ask which acid family you are comparing first, then apply that family's rule. The most common exam mistake is using electronegativity down a group: F is the most electronegative halogen, yet HF is the weakest hydrohalic acid.

Why

Down a group, the bond to H gets dramatically weaker (H–F about 567 kJ/mol, H–I about 299 kJ/mol), and that effect outweighs the drop in electronegativity. Across a row, atom size barely changes, so electronegativity becomes the deciding factor. In oxyacids, each extra O or a more electronegative Y pulls electron density away from the O–H bond (an inductive effect), making it more polar and easier to break. The extra O atoms also spread the negative charge of the anion over more atoms (resonance), which stabilizes the conjugate base. A more stable conjugate base is a weaker base, and therefore belongs to a stronger acid.

Conjugate bases and solvents (from your handout)

  • Acid strength = ease of removing a proton. Base strength = availability of nonbonded (lone) electron pairs.
  • Conjugate bases of binary acids: basicity decreases F− > Cl− > Br− > I−, because a larger ionic radius spreads (diffuses) the electron cloud, so the lone pairs are held less available.
  • Across a row: NH2− > OH− > F− in basicity (decreasing availability of lone pairs), mirroring NH3 < H2O < HF in acidity. Stronger acid = weaker conjugate base.
  • Conjugate bases of oxyacids: ClO− > ClO2− > ClO3− > ClO4− in basicity. More resonance structures spread the negative charge and make a more stable, weaker base.
Leveling

The leveling effect is a solvent's ability to level the strength of strong acids or bases dissolved in it. HClO4 has more O atoms and is intrinsically more acidic than HCl, but in water both ionize completely to H3O+, so they look equally strong.

  1. A strong acid is leveled to the strength of the solvent's conjugate acid (H3O+ in water).
  2. A strong base is leveled to the strength of the solvent's conjugate base (OH− in water).
  3. Strongly basic solvents level acids; weakly basic solvents are differentiating solvents that reveal differences between strong acids.

Your handout gives NH3 as the solvent that differentiates HI > HCl. By its own rule 3, though, NH3 is more basic than water and would level acids even more. The usual textbook example of a differentiating solvent for strong acids is a weakly basic one such as pure acetic acid. If this comes up, it's worth confirming with your instructor which example they expect.

Lesson 3

How

Worked example: pH of 0.10 M NaF (Ka of HF = 6.8 × 10−4)

Na+ is a spectator. F− is the conjugate base of a weak acid, so it is basic.

Kb(F−) = 1.0 × 10−14 / 6.8 × 10−4 = 1.47 × 10−11.

x = √(1.47 × 10−11 × 0.10) = 1.21 × 10−6 M OH− → pOH 5.92 → pH = 8.08.

Worked example: pH of 0.10 M NH4Cl (Kb of NH3 = 1.8 × 10−5)

Cl− is a spectator. NH4+ is the conjugate acid of a weak base: Ka = 1.0 × 10−14 / 1.8 × 10−5 = 5.6 × 10−10.

x = √(5.6 × 10−10 × 0.10) = 7.5 × 10−6 M H3O+ → pH = 5.13.

Both ions hydrolyze: NH4CN, NH4F, NH4CH3COO

Ka(NH4+) = 5.6 × 10−10 in all three. Compare it with the anion's Kb:

  • CN−: Kb = 10−14/4.9 × 10−10 = 2.0 × 10−5 > 5.6 × 10−10 → NH4CN is basic.
  • F−: Kb = 1.5 × 10−11 < 5.6 × 10−10 → NH4F is acidic.
  • CH3COO−: Kb = 5.6 × 10−10 = Ka → ammonium acetate is about neutral.
  • Your handout uses Ka(HCN) = 6.2 × 10−10, so Kb(CN−) = 1.6 × 10−5. Same conclusion: still far larger than 5.6 × 10−10, so NH4CN is basic.
Amphiprotic anions: HCO3−, H2PO4−, HPO42−

An amphiprotic ion can either lose a proton (its Ka is the next Ka of the parent acid) or gain one (its Kb = Kw/the previous Ka). Whichever constant is bigger wins.

  • HCO3−: Ka = Ka2 = 5.6 × 10−11; Kb = 10−14/Ka1(4.3 × 10−7) = 2.3 × 10−8. Kb wins → baking soda is basic. pH ≈ ½(pKa1 + pKa2) = ½(6.37 + 10.25) ≈ 8.3.
  • H2PO4−: Ka = Ka2 = 6.2 × 10−8; Kb = 10−14/7.5 × 10−3 = 1.3 × 10−12 → NaH2PO4 is acidic.
  • HPO42−: Ka = Ka3 = 4.2 × 10−13; Kb = 10−14/6.2 × 10−8 = 1.6 × 10−7 → Na2HPO4 is basic.
Why

Why spectators stay silent. Cl− is the conjugate base of a strong acid. Its Kb would be Kw/(huge Ka), which is effectively zero, so it never pulls a proton from water. The same logic makes Na+ inert: NaOH is strong, so Na+ has no tendency to grab OH− back.

Why some metal ions are acidic. In water, a metal cation is surrounded by water molecules bonded through oxygen (a Lewis acid–base interaction). A small ion with a high charge, like Al3+ or Fe3+, has a high charge density. It pulls electron density out of the O–H bonds of its attached waters, which weakens them until one releases H+. [Al(H2O)6]3+ has Ka ≈ 1.4 × 10−5, about as strong as acetic acid. Na+ is too large and its charge too low to do this. The higher the charge and the smaller the radius, the more acidic the hydrated ion.

Local linkTawas (alum, KAl(SO4)2·12H2O), sold as a deodorant and used in water treatment, makes acidic solutions because of hydrated Al3+. In treatment plants, Al3+ hydrolysis forms gelatinous Al(OH)3, which traps suspended particles so they settle out. Baking soda works as an antacid because HCO3− is basic.

Lesson 4

The common-ion effect

What

The common-ion effect is the shift of an ionic equilibrium caused by adding a solute that supplies an ion already in the equilibrium. Adding the common ion pushes the equilibrium toward the un-ionized (or undissolved) side.

  • Weak acid + its salt: CH3COOH + CH3COONa. The extra CH3COO− suppresses ionization, so [H3O+] drops and pH rises.
  • Weak base + its salt: NH3 + NH4Cl. The extra NH4+ suppresses ionization, so [OH−] drops and pH falls.
  • Sparingly soluble salt + a soluble salt sharing an ion: AgCl in NaCl solution. The extra Cl− lowers how much AgCl dissolves (Lesson 12).
How

Put the common ion into the Initial row of the ICE table. That one change is the whole technique.

Compare: without acetate, pH = 2.87 and percent ionization = 1.3%. With acetate, percent ionization = 1.8 × 10−5/0.10 = 0.018%, about 75 times less.

[OH−] = Kb[NH3]/[NH4+] = 1.8 × 10−5 × 0.20/0.30 = 1.2 × 10−5 M → pOH 4.92 → pH = 9.08. Plain 0.20 M NH3 would be pH 11.28.

Why

This is Le Châtelier's principle with a number attached. Adding A− makes Q = [H3O+][A−]/[HA] larger than Ka. To bring Q back down to Ka, the reaction runs in reverse, consuming H3O+ and A− to form HA. Ka itself does not change (only temperature changes K); what changes is the position of the equilibrium.

The common-ion effect also explains why the "x is small" approximation becomes excellent: the common ion already suppresses x, so 0.10 ± x really is 0.10. And it is the direct foundation of buffers. A solution with large amounts of both HA and A− has a pH set by their ratio, not by how far HA ionizes. Lesson 9 builds on exactly this.

Handout: H2S and added HCl

H2S(aq) ⇌ 2H+(aq) + S2−(aq). Adding HCl raises [H+] sharply. H+ and S2− recombine into undissociated H2S, so [S2−] drops dramatically. Because [S2−] depends on [H+]2, a small change in pH changes [S2−] enormously. Chemists use this in qualitative analysis to control which metal sulfides precipitate.

Not a common ionAdding NaCl to acetic acid adds no common ion, so it has essentially no effect on the acetic acid equilibrium at this level. Adding HCl does supply a common ion (H3O+), and it suppresses acetic acid ionization even more strongly than acetate does.

Lesson 5

Buffers

What

A buffer resists changes in pH when small amounts of strong acid or strong base are added. It contains a weak acid and its conjugate base (CH3COOH/CH3COO−) or a weak base and its conjugate acid (NH3/NH4+), both in appreciable amounts.

Henderson–Hasselbalch:   pH = pKa + log ( [base] / [acid] )
  • When [base] = [acid], pH = pKa.
  • Effective range: pKa ± 1, which is where the base/acid ratio is between 1:10 and 10:1.
  • Buffer capacity is how much acid or base it can absorb before the pH changes much. It depends on the absolute amounts of HA and A−, not just their ratio, and it is highest when the ratio is 1:1.
  • Not buffers: strong acid + its salt (HCl + NaCl), or a weak acid alone. A strong acid's conjugate (Cl−) cannot neutralize added H+, and a weak acid alone has no base reservoir.
How

Choosing a buffer: pick a weak acid whose pKa is closest to the target pH, then set the ratio with Henderson–Hasselbalch.

Adding strong acid or base is a two-step problem. Step 1: stoichiometry, in moles. The strong reagent reacts completely. Step 2: equilibrium, using Henderson–Hasselbalch with the new moles (the volume cancels in the ratio).

Worked example: add 0.010 mol HCl to 1.00 L of 0.25 M CH3COOH / 0.25 M CH3COONa

Start: pH = 4.74 + log(0.25/0.25) = 4.74.

Stoichiometry: H+ + CH3COO− → CH3COOH. Acetate 0.25 → 0.24 mol; acetic acid 0.25 → 0.26 mol.

Equilibrium: pH = 4.74 + log(0.24/0.26) = 4.74 − 0.035 = 4.71. It changed by only 0.03.

The same 0.010 mol HCl in 1.00 L of pure water gives pH 2.00, a drop of 5 units.

Worked example: what ratio gives pH 5.00 with acetic acid?

5.00 = 4.74 + log(b/a) → log(b/a) = 0.26 → b/a = 100.26 = 1.8. You need 1.8 mol of acetate for every mole of acetic acid.

Why

A buffer has a reservoir on each side. Added H+ is captured by the base (A− + H+ → HA). Added OH− is captured by the acid (HA + OH− → A− + H2O). The strong reagent is converted into the weak partner, and pH depends only on the logarithm of a ratio. With large amounts of both, a small addition barely changes the ratio, and the log flattens that change even further. Henderson–Hasselbalch is just Ka rearranged: take −log of [H3O+] = Ka[HA]/[A−] and you get it. The ±1 range follows because outside 10:1 one reservoir becomes too small to absorb anything.

Diluting a buffer with water does not change its pH (the ratio is unchanged), but it does lower its capacity.

For a basic buffer (weak base + its salt), your handout uses the pOH form: pOH = pKb + log([conjugate acid]/[weak base]), then pH = 14 − pOH. The two assumptions behind both forms: [weak acid] ≈ concentration of acid used (it barely dissociates), and [conjugate base] ≈ concentration of salt used (the salt fully dissociates, and the acid adds negligible A−).

Handout examples: three buffer calculations

3.20 g sodium ethanoate (Mr 82) in 1.00 L of 0.01 M ethanoic acid, Ka = 1.75 × 10−5: [salt] = 3.20/82 = 0.039 M. pH = −log(1.75 × 10−5) + log(0.039/0.01) = 4.76 + 0.59 = 5.35.

300 mL of 0.40 M NH3 + 200 mL of 0.45 M NH4Cl, Kb = 1.8 × 10−5: after mixing (500 mL), [NH3] = 0.40 × 300/500 = 0.24 M and [NH4+] = 0.45 × 200/500 = 0.18 M. pOH = 4.74 + log(0.18/0.24) = 4.62, so pH = 9.38. Dilute both to the final volume, or just use moles: the volume cancels.

500 mL of 2.0 M butanoic acid + 500 mL of 0.50 M sodium butanoate (Ka = 1.50 × 10−5), then 100 mL of 0.50 M HCl: moles: acid 1.0, base 0.25. Before: pH = 4.82 + log(0.25/1.0) = 4.22. HCl adds 0.05 mol H+: base 0.25 → 0.20, acid 1.0 → 1.05. After: pH = 4.82 + log(0.20/1.05) = 4.10. Change = 0.12, only a small change, as a buffer should give.

Polyprotic acids

Beyond your handoutYour Ionic Equilibria handout only touches polyprotic acids through H2SO4, H3PO4 titration and H2S. This lesson is extra background that CHEM 18 exams often include. Skim it if time is short.
What

A polyprotic acid has more than one ionizable proton and loses them one at a time, each step with its own Ka.

How
  • If successive Ka values differ by about 10³ or more, nearly all the H3O+ comes from the first step. Treat the acid as monoprotic using Ka1.
  • For a diprotic acid H2A, the concentration of A2− ≈ Ka2, regardless of the starting concentration.

Worked example: 0.10 M H2CO3 — find pH, [HCO3−], [CO32−]

Step 1: x = √(4.3 × 10−7 × 0.10) = 2.1 × 10−4 M = [H3O+] = [HCO3−]. pH = 3.68.

Step 2: Ka2 = [H3O+][CO32−]/[HCO3−]. Since [H3O+] ≈ [HCO3−], they cancel, so [CO32−] = Ka2 = 5.6 × 10−11 M.

Why

Why Ka1 > Ka2 > Ka3, always. The first proton leaves a neutral molecule. The second must leave an ion that is already negative, and the third leaves a 2− ion. Pulling a positive proton away from an increasingly negative species takes more and more energy, because of electrostatic attraction. On top of that, the H3O+ already produced by step 1 is a common ion that suppresses step 2. Both effects push the later steps far to the left.

The species present depends on pH relative to each pKa. At pH = pKa1, [H2A] = [HA−]. Between pKa1 and pKa2, HA− dominates. Above pKa2, A2− dominates. The speciation simulator in Remember lets you watch this happen.

What

In a titration, a solution of known concentration (the titrant) is added to an unknown (the analyte) until the equivalence point, where moles of acid equal moles of base by the balanced equation. The end point is where the indicator changes color. A good indicator makes the two nearly coincide.

Every point on a weak acid–strong base curve falls in one of four regions, and each region uses a different method:

  1. Before any base: weak-acid ICE problem (Lesson 4).
  2. Between start and equivalence: a buffer. Stoichiometry, then Henderson–Hasselbalch. At the half-equivalence point, [HA] = [A−], so pH = pKa.
  3. At equivalence: only A− is left. Solve it as a weak base, using the total volume (Lesson 7).
  4. After equivalence: excess OH− from the strong base controls pH. Divide excess moles by total volume.

Polyprotic titrations show one equivalence point per proton (if the Ka values are far enough apart). H3PO4 shows two clear jumps; the third is too weak to see in water. At the first half-equivalence point pH = pKa1; at the second, pH = pKa2.

Why

The curve is flat in the buffer region because you are making a buffer as you go: every drop of OH− converts HA into A−. It is flattest at half-equivalence, where the 1:1 ratio gives maximum buffer capacity. Near equivalence, the HA reservoir runs out, so the next drops have nothing to react with and the pH shoots up.

An indicator is itself a weak acid, HIn, whose acid and base forms have different colors. It changes color across roughly pKIn ± 1. So you choose an indicator whose range falls inside the steep jump at the equivalence point. The weaker the acid being titrated, the higher the equivalence pH and the smaller the jump. That is why very weak acids (Ka < ~10−8) cannot be titrated accurately with a color indicator.

Solubility equilibria and Ksp

What

An ionic solid labeled insoluble still dissolves a little, and the saturated solution reaches an equilibrium with the solid. The solubility product Ksp describes it. The solid is left out of the expression.

CaF2(s) ⇌ Ca2+(aq) + 2 F−(aq)    Ksp = [Ca2+][F−]2 = 3.9 × 10−11
  • Molar solubility (s): mol of solid that dissolve per liter of saturated solution. Solubility may also be given in g/L.
  • Ksp is a constant at a given temperature. Solubility is not; it changes with common ions, pH and complex formation.

Worked example: mix equal volumes of 0.010 M BaCl2 and 0.010 M Na2SO4 (Ksp BaSO4 = 1.1 × 10−10)

Mixing halves each: [Ba2+] = [SO42−] = 0.0050 M. Q = (0.0050)² = 2.5 × 10−5 ≫ 1.1 × 10−10, so BaSO4 precipitates.

(a) Equal volumes of 1 × 10−3 M Ca2+ and SO42−: each becomes 5 × 10−4 M. Q = 2.5 × 10−7, below Ksp (2 × 10−5), so no precipitate.

(b) 25 mL of 0.01 M Ca2+ + 50 mL of 0.02 M SO42−: [Ca2+] = 3.33 × 10−3, [SO42−] = 0.0133 M. Q = 4.43 × 10−5 > 2 × 10−5, so CaSO4 precipitates until Q falls to Ksp.

Precipitation starts when [Ag+][OH−] = Ksp: [OH−] = 1.5 × 10−8/0.010 = 1.5 × 10−6 M, pOH = 5.82, pH ≈ 8.2 (the handout rounds to 8.17). At this pH the solution is just saturated; keep the pH below it to prevent AgOH from forming.

Handout: selective precipitation of Au+ and Ag+ (0.0010 M each) with NaCl. Ksp: AgCl 1.8 × 10−10, AuCl 2.0 × 10−13

Same formula type, so the lower Ksp precipitates first: AuCl.

[Cl−] to start AuCl: 2.0 × 10−13/0.0010 = 2.0 × 10−10 M. To start AgCl: 1.8 × 10−10/0.0010 = 1.8 × 10−7 M.

(a) Same formula type; CaCO3 has the lower Ksp, so it precipitates first.

(b) 0.10 mol Ca2+ meets 0.10 mol CO32−. At saturation [Ca2+] = √(1.69 × 10−8) = 1.3 × 10−4 M remains, so CaCO3 precipitated = 0.10 − 0.00013 = 0.0999 mol.

Does CaSO4 also precipitate? Q = (1.3 × 10−4)(0.10) = 1.3 × 10−5 < 2.3 × 10−4, so no.

Selective precipitation: 0.010 M Cl− and 0.010 M CrO42−, add Ag+ slowly

AgCl begins when [Ag+] = 1.8 × 10−10/0.010 = 1.8 × 10−8 M. Ag2CrO4 (Ksp 1.1 × 10−12) begins when [Ag+] = √(1.1 × 10−12/0.010) = 1.0 × 10−5 M. AgCl precipitates first. The red Ag2CrO4 appears only after nearly all Cl− is gone, which is how the Mohr method detects the end point of a chloride titration.

Why

Compare Ksp values directly only for the same formula type. Ag2CrO4 has a smaller Ksp (1.1 × 10−12) than AgCl (1.8 × 10−10) but is more soluble: s = (Ksp/4)1/3 = 6.5 × 10−5 M versus 1.3 × 10−5 M. The exponents in the expression differ, so the constants are not on the same scale.

pH changes solubility when the anion is basic. In Mg(OH)2, CaCO3, CaF2 or Ca3(PO4)2, added H+ removes the anion (OH− → H2O, CO32− → HCO3− → CO2). Q drops below Ksp, so more solid dissolves. Salts of strong-acid anions (AgCl, BaSO4) are barely affected by pH, because Cl− has no basicity to remove.

Complexing agents (your handout): a metal complex forms from a metal ion (a Lewis acid) and ligands (Lewis bases). Its equilibrium constant is the formation (stability) constant, Kf = [Ag(NH3)2+]/[Ag+][NH3]2. Adding a complexing agent keeps a metal cation from precipitating, because the complex is more soluble than the metal salt.

0.0050 M AgNO3 in 2.00 M NH3 (Kf = 1.7 × 107): essentially all Ag+ becomes complex (0.0050 M), leaving 1.99 M NH3. Then Kd = 1/Kf = x(1.99)2/0.0050 gives [Ag+] = 7.4 × 10−11 M.

AgBr in (a) water and (b) 0.10 M NH3 (Ksp = 5.0 × 10−13, Kf = 1.7 × 107): (a) s = √(5.0 × 10−13) = 7.1 × 10−7 M. (b) Add the equations: AgBr(s) + 2NH3 ⇌ Ag(NH3)2+ + Br−, K = Ksp × Kf = 8.5 × 10−6 = x2/(0.10 − 2x)2, so x = 2.9 × 10−4 M, about 400 times more soluble.

Complex ions also raise solubility. AgCl dissolves in aqueous NH3 because Ag+ + 2NH3 ⇌ Ag(NH3)2+ (Kf = 1.7 × 107) removes free Ag+. Amphoteric hydroxides such as Al(OH)3, Zn(OH)2 and Cr(OH)3 dissolve in both strong acid and excess strong base, the latter by forming complexes like Al(OH)4−.

The big picture: every problem is two moves

Move 1 · Stoichiometry (goes to completion)

  • Strong electrolytes split completely: NaF → Na+ + F−; K2CO3 → 2K+ + CO32−.
  • Strong acid or strong base + anything reacts completely: H3O+ + A− → HA; OH− + HA → A− + H2O.
  • Mixing and dilution change concentrations, never moles: C1V1 = C2V2.
  • Reactions with huge K (complex formation, Kf ~107+) also go "to completion first".

Work in mol or mmol here. Volumes come back only when you need concentrations.

Move 2 · Equilibrium (shifts only a little)

  • Whatever weak species is left sets up one controlling equilibrium: Ka, Kb, Ksp, Kf, or a sum of them.
  • Write it, write K (pure solids and liquids excluded), then an ICE table or a shortcut.
  • The change x is usually small: check it.

Every constant here is the law of mass action. KaKb = Kw, and Ksp × Kf for dissolving with a ligand, both come from adding reactions (constants multiply).

Why this order matters: a strong acid or base reacts essentially 100%, so its "equilibrium" is just a limiting-reagent problem. If you put a strong acid into an ICE table with a weak acid's Ka, the answer will be wrong. Finish everything that goes to completion first, then let the weak equilibrium adjust.

The seven-step solving routine

  1. List the species actually present. Split soluble salts into ions; cross out spectators (Na+, K+, Cl−, NO3−, ClO4−, Br−, I− are spectators in acid–base problems). Convert "0.10 mol in 500 mL" to molarity (0.20 M) now.
  2. Do the complete reactions. Mixing? Dilute each to the total volume. Strong acid/base added? Subtract in mmol, find what's left over. Big Kf? Form the complex first.
  3. Classify what's left using the table below: strong acid/base excess, weak acid, weak base, salt, buffer, sparingly soluble salt, complex. This picks the formula.
  4. Write the controlling equilibrium and its K. Get the right constant: Kb = Kw/Ka for a conjugate base; K = KspKf for dissolving with a ligand. Check the stoichiometric exponents.
  5. ICE or shortcut. Define x as something you need (often [H3O+], [OH−] or s). Plug in starting values, including common ions.
  6. Check the approximation. x/C < 5%? If not, solve the quadratic. Is x near 10−7 M? Include water. Is the solubility physically impossible (> ~10 M)? Interpret it ("completely soluble").
  7. Convert and check. To pH/pOH, %, grams, mL, as asked. Run the sanity checks: acid < 7, base > 7, buffer within pKa ± 1, common ion lowers solubility, etc. Report sig figs (pH decimals = sig figs of [H3O+]).

Checklists by problem type

Open the one you need. Each lists the steps, what to keep in mind, the traps that cost the most points, and a quick check of your answer.

Strong acids and bases (incl. dilution and mixing)

Steps. (1) Count H+ or OH− per formula unit. (2) Mixing acid and base: mmol acid − mmol base → excess ÷ total volume. (3) pH = −log[H3O+] or 14 − pOH.

Keep in mind. A 10× dilution of a strong acid raises pH by exactly 1. At equivalence of strong + strong, pH = 7.00.

TrapsForgetting the 2 in Ba(OH)2; reporting pOH as pH; using the original volume instead of the total after mixing; 10−8 M HCl → "pH 8" (it's 6.98, water dominates).

CheckAcid pH < 7, base > 7. Dilution moves pH toward 7, never past it.

Weak acid: pH, [H3O+], percent ionization, Ka from pH

Steps. (1) HA ⇌ H+ + A−; Ka = x2/(C − x). (2) Try x = √(KaC). (3) 5% check: x/C × 100. (4) Fails → quadratic: x = [−Ka + √(Ka2 + 4KaC)]/2. (5) Convert. Ka from pH: x = 10−pH, then Ka = x2/(C − x).

Keep in mind. Quick predictor: if C/Ka > 400, the shortcut will pass. Percent ionization ≈ √(Ka/C): it rises on dilution.

TrapsUsing √(KaC) when a salt of the acid is present (that's a buffer); dropping x when the check fails; computing Ka from pH but forgetting C − x in the denominator.

CheckWeak acid pH is higher than a strong acid of the same C. % ionization < 100% and smaller at higher C.

Weak base and basic/acidic salts (hydrolysis)

Steps. (1) Identify which ion came from a weak parent. (2) Anion of weak acid → base: Kb = Kw/Ka. Cation of weak base → acid: Ka = Kw/Kb. (3) x = √(KC). (4) Convert; for a base, pOH first then pH = 14 − pOH.

Keep in mind. The weaker the parent acid, the stronger (more basic) its salt. Very dilute salt (x ≲ 10−6 M): add water, [OH−] ≈ √(KbC + Kw).

TrapsUsing Ka directly for the anion; calling NaF "neutral"; getting pH < 7 for a basic salt (water was ignored); forgetting the metal cation of a weak base (NH4+) is acidic.

CheckBasic salt pH ≥ 7, acidic salt ≤ 7. A 100× more concentrated salt shifts pH one unit further from 7.

Common-ion effect and buffer composition

Steps. (1) Recognize both HA and A− present (from a salt or partial neutralization). (2) [H3O+] = Ka[HA]/[A−] or pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA]). (3) For a base buffer: [OH−] = Kb[B]/[BH+], or use pKa of BH+ = 14 − pKb.

Keep in mind. Ratio only: mol/mol works because both are in the same volume. Dilution doesn't change buffer pH; it lowers capacity.

TrapsFlipping the ratio (it's Base Over Acid); using concentrations from before a reaction with added strong acid/base; treating the salt as if it also ionized only partly (it dissociates completely).

CheckMore acid than base → pH < pKa; more base → pH > pKa. Answer within pKa ± 1 for a useful buffer.

Buffer design (grams of salt for a target pH)

Steps. (1) Check target pH is within pKa ± 1. (2) log ratio = pH − pKa → ratio = 10(pH − pKa). (3) [A−] = ratio × [HA]. (4) mol = [A−] × V (in L). (5) grams = mol × MW.

Keep in mind. Use the unrounded pKa. Assume no volume change unless told otherwise.

TrapsForgetting to multiply by the volume (0.500 L, not 1 L); using the acid's MW instead of the salt's; inverting the ratio (your ratio must be > 1 if pH > pKa).

CheckPlug your grams back: mol salt/mol acid → log → + pKa should return the target pH.

Buffer after adding strong acid or base

Steps. (1) Moles of HA, A− and the added H3O+/OH−. (2) React to completion: OH− converts HA → A−; H3O+ converts A− → HA. (3) New ratio → H–H.

TrapsAdding the strong acid's concentration straight to [H3O+]; skipping the stoichiometry and putting both into H–H; not checking if the buffer was exhausted (then it's an excess strong acid/base problem).

CheckpH should move only slightly (≈ 0.1) in the direction of what you added.

Titration analysis: finding a sample's concentration (aliquots, dilution)

Steps. (1) Sketch the path: original → diluted to Vf → aliquot Va → titrated. (2) mol titrant = M × V. (3) Mole ratio (1 : 1 for HCl/NaOH or CH3COOH/NaOH; 1 : 2 for H2SO4/NaOH). (4) [analyte in aliquot] = mol/Va. (5) × (Vf/Vsample) for each dilution.

Keep in mind. Strength doesn't matter: weak acids still react completely with OH−. Mass % = (mol/L × MW)/(density × 1000) × 100.

TrapsConfusing the sample volume with the aliquot volume; forgetting to undo the dilution; using SF incorrectly for polyprotic acids; rounding to 2 s.f. when data have 4.

CheckThe original must be more concentrated than the diluted solution, by exactly the dilution factor.

Titration curves: pH at any point

Steps. (1) Veq from moles. (2) Which region? 0 mL → weak acid alone; before Veq → buffer (H–H with mmol); exactly Veq → conjugate base alone (Kb = Kw/Ka, total volume!); after Veq → excess OH− ÷ total volume.

TrapsAssuming pH 7 at equivalence for a weak acid; forgetting added volume at equivalence; using H–H at 0 mL (no A− yet) or at equivalence (no HA left).

CheckHalf-equivalence pH = pKa. Weak acid/strong base equivalence > 7; weak base/strong acid < 7.

Polyprotic acids

Steps. (1) Treat as monoprotic using Ka1 for pH. (2) [HA−] ≈ [H3O+]. (3) [A2−] ≈ Ka2 (only when no other acid/base is present).

TrapsAdding Ka1 + Ka2; doubling [H3O+] for a diprotic weak acid; using [A2−] ≈ Ka2 when a strong acid is also present (then [A2−] = Ka1Ka2[H2A]/[H+]2).

Ksp ↔ molar solubility in water

Steps. (1) Balanced dissolution, solid on the left. (2) Ions in terms of s with coefficients: MaXb → as and bs. (3) Ksp = (as)a(bs)b. (4) Solve. g/L = s × MW.

Keep in mind. 1 : 1 → s2; 1 : 2 or 2 : 1 → 4s3; 1 : 3 → 27s4; 2 : 3 → 108s5.

TrapsWriting (s)(s) for a 1 : 2 salt; forgetting to raise the coefficient inside the parentheses; comparing solubility by Ksp across different formula types.

CheckBack-substitute s into Ksp.

Solubility with a common ion

Steps. (1) Initial concentration of the common ion from the soluble salt (count per formula!). (2) ICE: common ion = C0 + (b)s. (3) Assume s ≪ C0: s = Ksp/C0n (adjusting for coefficients). (4) Verify s/C0 < 5%. (5) Compare with water: fold decrease.

TrapsForgetting to raise the common ion to its exponent; using 0.010 M K2CO3 as 0.020 M carbonate (it's 0.010 M; the 2 is K+); thinking Ksp changed.

CheckSolubility with a common ion must be lower than in water.

Solubility at a fixed pH (hydroxides, carbonates, fluorides)

Steps. (1) "Buffered at pH …" means [H3O+] and [OH−] are fixed. (2) [OH−] = 10−(14 − pH). (3) s = [metal ion] = Ksp/[OH−]n. (4) Compare with the pH of the salt's own saturated solution.

Keep in mind. Below the natural saturated pH → more soluble; above it → less soluble (common ion). Each pH unit changes s by 10n for M(OH)n.

TrapsWriting (2s)2 for [OH−] when pH is fixed; using pH instead of pOH; refusing an absurdly large answer instead of interpreting it as "dissolves completely".

Dissolving a precipitate with a complexing ligand (Ksp × Kf)

Steps. (1) Add MX(s) ⇌ M+ + X− and M+ + nL ⇌ MLn+. (2) K = KspKf. (3) ICE: [MLn] = [X−] = s; [L] = L0 − ns. (4) For n = 2: √K = s/(L0 − 2s), linear in s.

Keep in mind. If K ≫ 1, the ligand is limiting: s approaches L0/n.

TrapsForgetting the coefficient 2 on NH3 in the ICE; dropping 2s from (L0 − 2s) when s is not small; dividing Ksp by Kf instead of multiplying.

CheckThe answer should be much larger than the solubility in pure water.

Will a precipitate form? (Q vs Ksp)

Steps. (1) Total volume. (2) Dilute each ion: C × Vown/Vtotal. (3) Q = [cation]a[anion]b. (4) Q > Ksp → precipitate; Q < Ksp → no precipitate; Q = Ksp → saturated.

TrapsUsing undiluted concentrations; forgetting exponents in Q.

Selective precipitation (which first, thresholds, what's left)

Steps. (1) Molarity of each ion (convert mol → M). (2) Threshold precipitant concentration for each salt: Ksp/[ion] for 1 : 1; √(Ksp/[ion]) when the precipitant is squared. (3) Lower threshold precipitates first. (4) When the second starts, [first ion] = Ksp(first)/[precipitant at 2nd threshold]n. (5) % remaining = left/initial × 100.

TrapsUsing moles instead of molarity; picking "smaller Ksp first" when formula types differ (Mohr: AgCl before Ag2CrO4); using the first threshold in step 4.

CheckThe remaining first ion should be far below its initial value if the Ksp values differ by ≳ 103.

Conceptual questions: a reasoning routine

  1. Name the equilibrium. Write the reaction the question is really about (ionization, hydrolysis, dissolution, complex formation). Most conceptual items are one reaction plus one disturbance.
  2. Ask what the change does to Q. Adding a product (common ion) → Q > K → shifts left. Removing a product (acid eating OH−, NH3 binding Ag+) → Q < K → shifts right. Diluting → favors the side with more dissolved particles.
  3. Separate the constant from the position. Ka, Kb, Kw, Ksp, Kf change only with temperature. Concentrations, pH, % ionization and solubility change with everything else.
  4. Separate strength from amount. Strong/weak is about K (fraction ionized). Concentrated/dilute is about C. A dilute strong acid can have higher pH than a concentrated weak one.
  5. Trace conjugates and parents. Strong acid ↔ negligible conjugate base; weak acid ↔ weak (not negligible) conjugate base; KaKb = Kw.
  6. For structure questions, use one cause. Binary acids: bond strength down a group, electronegativity across a period. Oxyacids: more O atoms or more electronegative central atom → more stable anion → stronger acid.
  7. Answer in "what, because" form. State the effect, then the mechanism (Le Châtelier, K relationship, or structure). One sentence each.

Classic conceptual traps

Tempting statementWhat's actually true
"Neutral means pH 7."Neutral means [H3O+] = [OH−]. pH 7 is neutral only at 25 °C.
"A common ion lowers Ksp."It lowers solubility; Ksp is unchanged.
"Smaller Ksp = less soluble."Only for the same formula type. AgCl (1.8 × 10−10) is less soluble than Ag2CrO4 (1.1 × 10−12).
"Diluting a weak acid lowers its % ionization."It raises it (≈ √(Ka/C)); [H3O+] falls but the fraction ionized grows.
"Diluting a buffer changes its pH."The ratio is unchanged, so pH stays (nearly) the same; capacity drops.
"Equivalence point = pH 7."Only for strong + strong. Weak acid + strong base → basic; weak base + strong acid → acidic.
"Weak acids need more base to titrate."Equivalence volume depends on moles only.
"Endpoint and equivalence point are the same."Equivalence is stoichiometric; endpoint is where the indicator changes. A good indicator makes them close.
"Salts are neutral."A salt is acidic, basic or neutral depending on whether its ions come from weak parents.
"Acid dissolves any precipitate."Only salts of basic anions (OH−, CO32−, F−, S2−, PO43−). AgCl doesn't dissolve in HNO3.
"pH measures acid strength."pH depends on strength and concentration; Ka/pKa measures strength.
"A diprotic acid makes twice the H3O+."For weak polyprotic acids the second step adds almost nothing (Ka2 ≪ Ka1).
"Pure solids belong in Ksp."Pure solids and liquids are left out (activity = 1); aqueous ions and gases are included.
"A stronger base buffer is better."The best buffer has pKa near the target pH and high concentrations of both components.
"Hot water is acidic because pH < 7."It's still neutral; Kw grew, so both ions increased equally.

Comparative (Column I vs II) questions: a reasoning routine

A: Column I greater · B: Column I less · C: equal · D: cannot be determined. The Compare tab has a shorter guide; this is the full routine.

  1. Name the quantity and its direction. Is it pH or [H3O+]? pKa or Ka? s or Ksp? pH, pOH and pK move opposite to the concentration or constant they come from. Write the arrow next to each column before deciding.
  2. Find what's the same and what differs. Same acid, different C? Same C, different Ka? Same salt, different solvent? The one thing that differs drives the answer.
  3. Pick the governing relation. pH ~ ½(pKa − log C); % ion ≈ √(Ka/C); pH = pKa + log ratio; s = √Ksp vs (Ksp/4)1/3; s ≈ Ksp/[common]; K = KspKf.
  4. If unsure, put rough numbers in. Order-of-magnitude arithmetic is enough. Example: s(AgCl) ≈ 10−5 vs s(Ag2CrO4) ≈ 10−4.
  5. Test the "equal" possibilities on purpose. Constants at the same T; ratio-only quantities (buffer pH after dilution); equivalence volumes for equal moles; pH at half-equivalence vs pKa; [A2−] vs Ka2; mol analyte vs mol titrant at equivalence.
  6. Choose D only when a missing variable can flip the answer. Not when it's just tedious. Ask: "Is there a value of the missing quantity that makes Column I bigger, and another that makes it smaller?" If yes → D.

Usually equal (C)

  • Ka, Ksp, Kw at the same T, whatever is added
  • Buffer pH before vs after dilution
  • Veq for strong vs weak acid with equal moles
  • pH at half-equivalence and pKa
  • KaKb of a conjugate pair and Kw
  • [H3O+] and [OH−] in pure water at any T
  • mol analyte and mol titrant (1 : 1) at equivalence

Direction traps

  • Larger Ka → lower pH and lower pKa
  • Weaker parent acid → higher salt pH
  • More dilute weak acid → higher % ionization, higher pH
  • More dilute basic salt → lower pH (closer to 7)
  • Common ion → lower s; complexing ligand or acid (basic anion) → higher s
  • Fixed pH below a hydroxide's natural pH → higher s; above → lower s

When D is right

  • pH of two different acids with concentrations not given
  • Solubility of salts of different formula type when Ksp values are not given
  • Buffer capacity when only the ratio is known
  • Which precipitates first when the starting ion concentrations aren't given and formula types differ

Sanity checks before you box the answer

pH

Acid < 7, base > 7. Strong acid pH = −log C. Weak acid pH higher than a strong acid of the same C. Buffer within pKa ± 1. Equivalence of weak acid > 7.

Percent and fractions

% ionization below 100% and below 5% if you used the shortcut. % remaining in selective precipitation tiny if Ksp values are far apart.

Solubility

Common ion → smaller than in water. Ligand or acid (basic anion) → larger. Above ~10 M → "completely soluble", say so.

Concentrations

Original sample more concentrated than its dilution. Free metal ion in excess ligand ≪ total metal. x below the starting concentration.

Significant figures

pH: decimals = s.f. in [H3O+] (4.8 × 10−3 → 2.32). Titration data with 4 s.f. → answer with 4. Don't round intermediate steps.

Units and volumes

mL ↔ L. mol vs M (0.10 mol in 0.500 L = 0.20 M). Total volume after mixing. Grams need × volume × MW.

Remember & visualize

Make it stick

Hooks for the lists you must memorize, flashcards for quick recall, and five small labs where you change something and watch the chemistry respond. Predict the result before you touch a control.

Memory hooks

The six strong acids (handout)

"Clara Brought Iced Sodas, No Peanuts"

HCl · HBr · HI · H2SO4 · HNO3 · HClO4 (Perchloric). Three hydrogen halides + three oxyacids. Some books add HClO3. HF is weak.

Strong bases

All of Group 1, plus "Ca·Sr·Ba"

Read it as "Can Sara Bake?" Group 2 hydroxides from Ca down. Mg(OH)2 and Be(OH)2 are too insoluble to count.

Henderson–Hasselbalch

B·O·A

Base Over Acid: pH = pKa + log(base/acid). More base pushes pH above pKa; more acid pulls it below.

Salts

"A salt takes after its stronger parent"

NH4Cl comes from weak NH3 and strong HCl, so it is acidic. NaF comes from strong NaOH and weak HF, so it is basic. Two strong parents: neutral. Two weak parents: compare Ka and Kb.

Conjugates

The seesaw: Ka × Kb = 10−14

One end goes up, the other goes down. A strong acid has a uselessly weak conjugate base; a very weak acid has a fairly strong one.

Half-equivalence

"Halfway there, pH is pKa"

Half the acid has become its conjugate base, the ratio is 1, log 1 = 0. This is how pKa is measured in the lab.

Indicator choice

Weak acid → pink · Weak base → red

Titrating a weak acid? The equivalence point is basic, so use phenolphthalein (turns pink). Titrating a weak base? It's acidic, so use methyl red.

Common-ion effect

"Add a product, push it back"

Supplying an ion that appears on the product side drives the equilibrium toward reactants: less ionization, less dissolving.

Polyprotic acids

"Each proton is harder to pull"

It leaves an ion that is more negative each time, so Ka1 ≫ Ka2 ≫ Ka3. First step sets the pH; [A2−] ≈ Ka2.

Ksp shortcuts

1:1 → s² · 1:2 → 4s³ · 1:3 → 27s⁴

Multiply the coefficient raised to its own power for each ion: (2s)² = 4s², times s = 4s³.

Precipitation

"Q over K, ions fall away"

If Q > Ksp, there are too many ions in solution; solid falls out until Q = Ksp.

Neutral water

"Neutral means equal, not seven"

pH 7 is neutral only at 25 °C. Warmer water has a lower neutral pH but is still neutral.
